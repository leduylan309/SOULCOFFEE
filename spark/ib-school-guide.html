In short

CEFR, in short

  • Six levels in three bands: A1–A2 Basic, B1–B2 Independent, C1–C2 Proficient.
  • ~200 guided learning hours to move up one full level — about one academic year of intensive study.
  • B2 is the minimum for IGCSE; strong B2–C1 for IB English B; C1–C2 for IB Language & Literature HL.
  • The B1 plateau is real: conversational fluency arrives in ~2 years, academic proficiency takes 5–7.

If your child attends an international or bilingual school in HCMC, you have almost certainly heard the term “CEFR” without anyone explaining what it actually means for your family. School reports mention it. English centers reference it. IELTS scores map to it. But most parents have never had CEFR explained in a way that connects to what their child is doing in class, what level they should be at, and what needs to happen next.

This guide fixes that. It explains what each CEFR level means in plain language, maps the levels to international school year groups, shows how CEFR connects to Cambridge Young Learners, IGCSE, IB English, and IELTS, and gives you a practical framework for understanding whether your child is where they should be.

What CEFR Is and Why It Matters

CEFR stands for the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, developed by the Council of Europe to describe language ability consistently across countries and systems. It has six levels, grouped into three bands:

A · Basic User

A1 Breakthrough · A2 Waystage. The child can handle very basic communication: introducing themselves, understanding simple questions, using familiar words and phrases.

B · Independent User

B1 Threshold · B2 Vantage. The child can function independently in English: understanding the main points of clear speech, dealing with most situations, producing connected text, expressing opinions.

C · Proficient User

C1 Advanced · C2 Mastery. The child can use English flexibly and effectively for any purpose: complex texts, fluent and precise expression, academic and professional communication.

CEFR is the common language connecting your child’s school curriculum, their English support, their exam preparation, and their university entrance requirements. Understanding it means you can make informed decisions instead of relying on vague assurances.

The Six CEFR Levels in Plain Language

A1

Breakthrough

Basic user

What your child can do

Introduce themselves. Say where they live. Name basic objects. Understand very simple instructions spoken slowly. Write their name and a few memorized phrases.

What this looks like in school

Can follow classroom routines (sit down, open your book) but cannot follow a lesson taught in English. Understands individual words but not sentences — functionally non-English-speaking in an academic context.

New arrivals with little prior EnglishCambridge: Pre-Starters
A2

Waystage

Basic user

What your child can do

Have simple conversations about daily routines, family, school, and hobbies. Understand short texts on familiar topics. Write simple sentences and short paragraphs about themselves.

What this looks like in school

Can participate in class at a basic level. Understands what is happening but struggles with extended listening. Writes sentences but not structured paragraphs. Year 1–3 for EAL students with one to two years of immersion.

Cambridge: Starters–Movers (YLE)IELTS ≈ 3.0–3.5
B1

Threshold

Independent user

What your child can do

Understand the main points of lessons and conversations on familiar topics. Produce connected text on subjects of personal interest. Describe experiences, express opinions, and give reasons.

What this looks like in school

Can follow most lessons but misses nuance, inference, and subject-specific vocabulary. Writing is coherent but lacks structure and accuracy. Year 3–6 for EAL students. This is the level where many students plateau.

Cambridge: Movers–Flyers / PETIELTS ≈ 4.0–5.0
B2

Vantage

Independent user

What your child can do

Understand complex texts, including academic material. Interact fluently without obvious searching for words. Produce clear, detailed writing. Argue a position, analyze a text, understand abstract ideas.

What this looks like in school

Can fully access the curriculum: structured essays, class discussions, reading at grade level. B2 is the expected level for beginning IGCSE study. Year 7–10.

Cambridge: FCE (B2 First)IELTS ≈ 5.5–6.5
C1

Advanced

Proficient user

What your child can do

Understand demanding, extended texts and recognize implicit meaning. Express themselves fluently and spontaneously. Produce well-structured, detailed text on complex subjects.

What this looks like in school

Excels academically in English: sophisticated essays, debates, IB Diploma or A-Level English. Year 10–13 for strong students.

Cambridge: CAE (C1 Advanced)IELTS ≈ 7.0–8.0
C2

Mastery

Proficient user

What your child can do

Understand virtually everything heard or read. Summarize information from diverse sources coherently. Express themselves spontaneously, very fluently, and with precision.

What this looks like in school

Near-native proficiency. Very few non-native speakers reach C2 before university — not a realistic target for most international school EAL students during their school years.

Cambridge: CPE (C2 Proficiency)IELTS ≈ 8.5–9.0

How Long It Takes to Move Between Levels

Cambridge English research estimates approximately 200 guided learning hours to move up one full CEFR level — roughly one academic year of intensive study, or two academic years of twice-weekly supplementary sessions. But this is an average, and individual variation is significant.

1Age

Younger children (under 8) acquire language faster in immersive environments but slower in structured classrooms. Older children learn grammar and vocabulary faster in structured settings but may have more fossilized errors to undo.

2First language

Vietnamese is a tonal, isolating language with no verb conjugation, no plural markers, and fundamentally different phonology. The transfer distance to English is longer than from European languages.

3Immersion hours

An English-medium international school gives 6–8 hours of immersion daily; a bilingual school 3–4. Total volume affects the rate of progression significantly.

4Quality of instruction

A child receiving ~1,000 corrections per year in a class of 6 consolidates accuracy faster than one receiving ~200 in a class of 20.

5The B1 plateau

Progress slows significantly at B1: children sound conversationally fluent but their academic English stops developing. A normal stage that requires deliberate, structured intervention.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions about CEFR levels

What CEFR level should my child be at for their age?

There is no single answer because progression depends on immersion hours, first language, quality of instruction, and aptitude. Rough guide: an EAL child who entered an English-medium international school at age 5 should be approaching B1 by age 9–10, and B2 by age 12–14. Native speakers are typically B2 by 10 and C1 by 14–16.

What CEFR level does my child need for IGCSE English?

B2 is the minimum for IGCSE English as a Second Language. B2+ to C1 is needed for English as a First Language. Children below B2 at the start of IGCSE preparation (Year 9–10) need targeted intervention.

What CEFR level does my child need for IB English?

IB English B (HL) requires strong B2 to C1. IB English Language and Literature (HL) requires C1 to C2. Students below B2 at the start of the Diploma (Year 12) will struggle significantly.

What CEFR level corresponds to IELTS 7.0?

Approximately the B2/C1 boundary. 7.5 is solidly C1; 8.0+ is C1+; 6.0 is upper B2; 5.0 is B1 to lower B2. Mappings are approximate.

My child sounds fluent but the school says they need EAL support. How?

Conversational fluency (BICS) develops in roughly 2 years of immersion. Academic language proficiency (CALP) takes 5–7 years. A child can be fluent at the playground and behind at the desk. CEFR captures both dimensions.

Can my child skip CEFR levels?

In practice, no. Language acquisition is sequential — you cannot write a coherent essay (B2) without writing a coherent paragraph (B1). What varies is speed. Intensive, structured, small-group instruction accelerates progression, but cannot skip stages.

How does Spark assess my child’s CEFR level?
